
My research aims to bridge theoretical modelling with real-world public health applications to address key questions relevant to disease transmission and control. By integrating approaches such as dynamical systems theory, Bayesian inference, optimal control, and machine learning, I aim to generate quantitative insights that can inform evidence-based public health decisions. I have designed and applied modelling frameworks to elucidate transmission mechanisms and optimise control strategies for multiple diseases, including dengue, cholera, and COVID-19.
I have developed mathematical models to investigate the spatial transmission dynamics of vector-borne and waterborne diseases in endemic settings. I designed a novel spatially explicit model for dengue transmission in Kolkata, India, an urban environment with high seasonal outbreaks. This model was used to quantify how neighbourhood-level connectivity shapes spread and assess the effectiveness of vector control measures. I have also developed a spatially structured transmission model to evaluate the impact of different control strategies across Zimbabwe’s provinces, accounting for spatial heterogeneity in intervention effectiveness.
During the COVID-19 pandemic, I contributed to multiple modelling efforts to assess outbreak severity, predict case trajectories, and optimize interventions. I developed a mechanistic model to estimate the severity of the first wave in India and predicted outbreak scenarios under varying intervention strengths. Complementing this, I applied machine learning methods to predict COVID-19 cases in India and other countries across the world, offering insights into short-term epidemic trends. I also developed a novel model-based framework that estimates cross-border human mobility during the COVID-19 pandemic using easily accessible data such as case counts, providing a practical solution particularly valuable for resource-limited settings lacking detailed movement data. The framework was applied in a case study of the Germany–Poland border to infer cross-border mobility patterns during COVID-19.
Managing infection risk within organizations emerged as a key challenge during the COVID-19 pandemic. In response, I collaborated on projects that integrated disease transmission models with optimization techniques to develop workplace occupancy strategies and testing protocols for long-term care facilities. These models incorporated epidemiological parameters and operational constraints to generate feasible, risk-minimizing solutions. This work also led to the development of two interactive dashboards, providing users with accessible, real-time solutions tailored to their specific operational contexts. These dashboards were used to support decision-making in several organizations, including retirement homes in Saxony, Germany.
My research also explores systematic understanding on the optimal resource allocation for epidemic control. I led the development of a theoretical modelling framework, motivated by real-world constraints to determines the optimal allocation of resources between transmission reduction (e.g., NPIs, vector control) and healthcare capacity strengthening (e.g., hospital infrastructure). I extended this framework to a spatial context through a case study on optimizing test-kit allocation during the COVID-19 pandemic, aiming to minimize cases and attack rates in resource-constrained settings.
Currently, as a Research Fellow at Saw Swee Hock School of Public Health (SSHSPH), I am leading the development of a mathematical modelling framework to assess dengue vaccination strategies under the supervision of Dr. Hannah Clapham (Co-PI). The framework explicitly integrates detailed features, including age structure, multi-serotype circulation, population exposure history to evaluate the potential impact of dengue vaccination in endemic settings. It has been applied primarily in the Singapore context and has generated policy-relevant evidence on the expected population impact of introducing a routine vaccination programme with the vaccine Qdenga (TAK-003). The model indicates that reductions in cases and hospitalisation depend on the predominant serotype and age group targeted for vaccination, and it provides scenario analyses intended to inform policy programme design under Singapore’s current dengue burden. It suggested that targeting older age groups will yield greater benefits than the 6-16-year window recommended by WHO-SAGE for high transmission settings.
I am leading an ongoing work using the same modelling framework to estimate the benefits of vaccination in high-transmission settings, beginning with Jakarta, Indonesia, which bears a substantial dengue burden. In parallel, I am supervising a project with a research intern to model dengue transmission among migrant workers in Singapore, applying the same framework to evaluate vaccination strategies for this higher-risk population.
I am also co-investigating a project evaluating the cost-effectiveness of dengue vaccination in Singapore and across ASEAN countries where the modelling framework that I developed is used as a robust dynamics transmission model based flatform. Given that many dengue-endemic countries are implementing Wolbachia releases, I am developing a general Wolbachia module within the framework, encompassing both suppression and replacement strategies to quantify their impacts on transmission, and to assess interactions with vaccination.
